# Plan 17, M1: the data for the stress of a pin through a pulse

Done 2026-10-20 for [plan 17](../plans/17-transient-mechanics.md) milestone M1. Reading only (open reports, fetched as PDFs and read as text or as page images); no product code. **No value here comes from an OFFBEAT file.** Where a value was read from a scan or a figure the note says so. A statement is marked **UNVERIFIED** when it comes from memory, a search snippet or a document that could not be opened.

**Result in one paragraph.** (1) The cold cladding now has two sourced strength tables below 400 C, in `library/strengths.json`: the Sandia slide curve (20 to 800 C, read from a figure, the same Carpenter/Crucible curves as the UT-Austin figure) and INL's measured 304L plate (21 to 316 C, three temperatures). They disagree with each other and with the slide's own words by 15 to 20 % in yield at 150 C, and none is measured on drawn TRIGA cladding tube: **the gate is met for the existence of a read source; the verdict it supports is coarse** (plan 17 D5 and D6, below). (2) 304 modulus against temperature has a primary measured source (ORNL-5442, a cubic polynomial, 24 to 871 C); the expansion has a tabulated measurement to 537 C (NUREG/CR-0481, from Touloukian) and **no read source from 537 to 800 C**. (3) Densities are found and read: U-ZrH1.6 5940 kg/m3 (Sandia) and 5990 to 6000 kg/m3 from Simnad's own formula; 304: 8000 kg/m3 (Sandia). The quasi-static check of plan 17 is **confirmed by arithmetic from read inputs**: a longitudinal wave crosses the 18.16 mm radius in 4.7 microseconds. (4) Hydrogen pressure in a pulse: the UT-Austin SAR (1991) and the NRC's evaluations use the **equilibrium** pressure at the **peak** temperature, with a safety factor; Simnad's own report says the real pressure in a step heating peaks at about **20 % of equilibrium after 0.3 s** and that a measured pulse gave **46 % of equilibrium**; so equilibrium at the peak is a stated upper bound, not a prediction. (5) The published TRIGA analyses compare **temperature** with limits, not a computed stress (only a Sandia finite-element study of a gapped variant at a steady 1000 C). (6) The fuel's own limits are three one-number design values from the UT-Austin SAR table.

## 2. Young's modulus and the expansion coefficient of 304

**Young's modulus.** ORNL-5442 (Hammond et al. 1979), type 304 plate, solution annealed, heat 9T2796, **dynamic** (sonic) modulus: `E = E0 [1 + A (T - T0) + B (T - T0)^2 + C (T - T0)^3]`, T in C, T0 = 24 C, E0 = 200.06 +- 0.32 GPa, A = -4.41980e-4, B = 3.07524e-9, C = 4.02024e-11 (Table 4, read as an image), standard deviation of the fit 0.35 GPa, **measured from room temperature to 871 C (1600 F)**. Evaluated: 200.1 GPa at 24 C, 193.4 at 100 C, 184.6 at 200 C, 175.9 at 300 C, 167.3 at 400 C, 159.0 at 500 C, 150.9 at 600 C, 143.1 at 700 C, 135.6 at 800 C, 130.5 at 871 C. The report's text says the dynamic E of the three heats varies little and E falls approximately linearly. Poisson's ratio (dynamic, same table): `nu = 0.29049 [1 + 3.54637e-4 x - 7.07393e-7 x^2 + 6.59136e-10 x^3]`, x = T - 24: 0.290 at 24 C, 0.303 at 200 C, 0.310 at 400 C, 0.318 at 600 C, 0.336 at 800 C. The report says the Poisson curves differ markedly between heats and "the effects displayed are not believed to be real": **a +-0.03 band is the honest uncertainty**.

**The static modulus polynomial of the same table was read as E0 = 196.27 GPa, A = -2.38802e-4, B = -1.10056e-8, C = 2.37683e-12, but evaluated it gives 158.8 GPa at 800 C, above the dynamic curve (135.6) from about 150 C up, while the text says the static curve lies below the dynamic one and approximately parallel to it (Fig. 17, not read).** I could not reconcile the two; the static polynomial is **not used** (a transcription or a definition I have not understood). At room temperature the report says the static E is about 3 % under the dynamic.

Cross-check, 1971 ASME Code values for annealed 304 as printed in NUREG/CR-0481 Table B-II (a degraded scan: the digits were read and checked for a smooth, monotone run): 191.4 GPa at 75 C, 184.0 at 200 C, 176.7 at 300 C, 168.3 at 400 C, 159.0 at 500 C, 149.1 at 600 C, 138.6 at 700 C. They agree with the ORNL dynamic curve to 2 % to 100 C and to 3 % at 700 C (ASME lower).

**What it means for the library:** the library's 190 GPa constant (a secondary source, no range) sits between 100 and 200 C on these curves; over the cladding's range in a pulse (20 to about 500 C) the modulus falls from about 200 to 159 GPa (**20 %**), and to 135 GPa at 800 C. For D3: the cladding modulus is a **law that matters at the 10 to 20 % level** over a pulse, the fuel modulus a law that matters at 34 % (62.7 to 41.4 GPa from 20 to 650 C, beyond 650 C **no source** for the hydride, and the pulse's peak fuel temperature, 824 C in the example, is past it).

**Thermal expansion.** NUREG/CR-0481, Appendix A, Table A-I (read as an image): the linear expansion of 304 (19.19 Cr, 8.49 Ni, 0.065 C; data from Touloukian et al. as the report cites them) as `dL/L0` in per cent against T, **233 to 810 K (-40 to 537 C)**, zero at about 293 K. From the table, the mean coefficient against 20 C: 16.0e-6 per K to 100 C, 16.6e-6 to 200 C, 17.1e-6 to 300 C, 17.6e-6 to 400 C, 18.0e-6 to 500 C (instantaneous about 16 at 100 C rising to about 19 to 20 at 500 C; a window of 90 K on 5 to 6 K steps, noisy at the 1e-6 level). The report's fit `dL/L0 (%) = c + 9.471e-4 T + 1.031e-6 T^2 - 2.978e-10 T^3`, T in K, **is printed with c = +0.358; with +0.358 it misses the table by 0.7 % (the value at 293 K would be 0.72 % instead of 0), with -0.358 it reproduces the table to 0.033 % at worst and 0.008 % on average** (a lost sign in the report or its scan; the fit is therefore stated here with -0.358 and checked, not trusted). The library's constant 17.2e-6 per K (secondary, no range) is the mean coefficient of this table for 20 to about 300 C; over a pulse (cladding to about 500 C) the mean rises to 18.0e-6 (**5 %**), and the instantaneous coefficient moves by 20 to 25 %. **No read source covers 537 to 800 C** (CINDAS Report 51 could not be opened; a search snippet puts 304 wire at 16.5e-6 per K at 100 C and 18.3e-6 per K at 500 C, **UNVERIFIED, a secondary paper, not used**).

The density at temperature is not stated for the fuel (its expansion over a pulse, 0.5 to 1 % in length, changes the density by 2 % at most). **A density can be a stated, sourced value in the case without a range.**

**The quasi-static assumption (plan 17 D2) is confirmed by arithmetic from read inputs.** For U-ZrH1.6 with E = 62.7 GPa (UT SAR Table 4-1, 20 C), nu = 0.32 and rho = 5940 to 5996 kg/m3 the one-dimensional rod speed is `sqrt(E/rho)` = 3.23 to 3.25 km/s and the longitudinal wave speed in a bulk solid, `sqrt(E (1 - nu) / (rho (1 + nu) (1 - 2 nu)))`, is **3.87 to 3.89 km/s**. The wave crosses the 18.161 mm radius in **4.7 microseconds** (about 5.6 microseconds at the slower rod speed; with the modulus 41.4 GPa at 650 C, about 6 to 7 microseconds). The example pulse's fastest change is milliseconds (FWHM 11.4 ms): about **2000 transit times across a rise time of a few ms**. The plan's unverified 6 microseconds is therefore **sound**. The stress wave in the cladding (0.508 mm thick, steel at 5.7 km/s) is a non-issue.

## 4. The hydrogen pressure of U-ZrH in a pulse

**What the SARs and the NRC assume.**

- UT-Austin SAR 1991, section 4.3.1 (page 4-54, read as an image): "The safety limit on fuel temperature occurring in the pulse mode of operation is 1150 C. This temperature will give an internal **equilibrium** hydrogen pressure (U-ZrH fuel H/Zr; 1.6) less than that which would produce a stress equivalent to the ultimate strength of the clad at a temperature of 680 C. This clad temperature is higher than would actually occur and therefore conservative even in the case of a pulse producing a peak adiabatic fuel temperature of 1150 C." And section 4.1 (page 4-1): "Fuel temperature limits of 1150 C (with clad <= 500 C) and 970 C (with clad >= 500 C) for U-ZrH (H/Zr; 1.65) have been set to preclude the loss of clad integrity" (**970 C in this 1991 SAR, not the 950 C of Simnad's report and of the plan's brief; the 2023 SAR was not re-read for this**). So **the SAR uses the equilibrium pressure at the peak (adiabatic) fuel temperature and compares the resulting hoop stress with the ultimate strength of the cladding**, with a stated conservatism in the cladding temperature. It computes no transient pressure and no thermal-gradient stress.
- NRC, Amendment 11 to R-106 (Technical Specification 2.1, read): the limit is "based on data which indicate that the stress in the cladding due to the hydrogen pressure from the dissociation of zirconium hydride will remain below the ultimate stress provided the temperature of the fuel does not exceed" 1150 C (FLIP) or 1000 C (standard) "and the fuel cladding is water cooled".
- NRC, Amendment 12 to R-76 (WSU, read): "The equilibrium hydrogen pressure of the fuel during a pulse is related to the temperature. By limiting temperature, pressure is limited." Fuel damage was seen above 874 C, "an equilibrium hydrogen pressure of about 70 psi"; a **safety factor of two in equilibrium pressure** gives 35 psi and an 830 C limit, accepted by the NRC; the damage after the Texas A&M event is explained by hydrogen **redistributed by long steady operation** ("migration from hot to cooler regions") so that a later pulse heats hydrogen-rich outer fuel (the pulse's peak is in the outer region).
- FEED's `hydrogenPressure.ts` (Simnad's correlation) reproduces the three numbers: X = 1.65 gives 2032 psi at 1150 C (Simnad prints 2080 psi), 66 psi at 874 C (NRC: about 70) and 32.5 psi at 830 C (NRC: 35), 2 to 6 % low, all from read documents (the NRC's X is not stated).

**What Simnad says about the equilibrium assumption (E-117-833, pages 3-1, 4-2 to 4-7, read).**

- Equilibrium at the maximum temperature "never occurs": the fuel is not at one temperature, and hydrogen released from the hot regions is taken up by the cooler ones. A diffusion calculation (Fig. 4-2, from Simnad, Foushee, West, Nucl. Technol. 28 (1976) 31, not read itself) after a **step** rise to a maximum fuel temperature of 1150 C: the internal pressure rises for **about 0.3 s**, reaches about **420 psi, 20 % of the equilibrium 2080 psi**, then falls as hydrogen redistributes along the element; steps to 1200 and 1350 C give about the same time and fraction. A step is "the most severe": a slower rise gives less, "the pressure will become increasingly less" as the rise time passes the hydrogen's diffusion time.
- Measured: "in a pulse in which the maximum temperature in the fuel was greater than 1000 C, the maximum pressure was only **46 % of the equilibrium value evaluated at the peak temperature**"; the analysis gave about three times the measured value. In the ACPR gapped element the gas pressure measured during pulses to 1000 C was "**less than 40 psia**", well below the upper bound implied by the equilibrium data (page 3-1). Elements pulsed to hot spots at 1175 to 1200 C swelled locally after 200 to 400 pulses.
- Mechanism of damage: bubbles of hydrogen nucleating in hot spots; the equilibrium pressure is an upper bound; creep and swelling of the fuel and bubbles are what matter, not a gas pressure in the gap.

**For D4.** The three forms of the plan stand, with the label made precise: (iii) the equilibrium pressure at the instantaneous temperature is **an upper bound in the SAR's sense (a factor 2 to 5 above the measured pressure in a pulse, from the sources above)**, not an equilibrium the fuel reaches in 10 ms: Simnad's own numbers put the pulse's real internal pressure at 20 to 46 % of equilibrium. **I did not find a measurement for a TRIGA pulse of a few milliseconds that gives a pressure against time**, nor the diffusion time constants (Simnad's Fig. 4-2 is read as a picture and its axes, 0.001 to 100 s, are all I took from it; Terrani et al. 2009 is not read). Suggested wording in the result: "equilibrium pressure at the instantaneous temperature: an upper bound that the SAR uses and that Simnad reports as 2 to 5 times the measured pulse pressure."

## 5. How published TRIGA analyses treat the cladding stress in a pulse

- The stress in the cladding is treated as a **thin-shell hoop stress from the gas pressure only**, `S = P r / t` (Simnad page 4-2, equation 1; the UT SAR's figure 4.3b uses a factor 36.7, in the library test). Example, Simnad page 4-2, ACPR cladding (r/t = 0.739/0.020 in), cladding at 138 C: the pressure that gives the yield stress, 38 ksi, is 1025 psi; that gives the ultimate, 68 ksi, is 1840 psi. At the 1150 C limit the equilibrium pressure is 5 to 18 times lower than the pressure needed to fail the cladding (page 4-7) for a cladding below 500 C.
- **No thermal-gradient stress is computed** in the SAR or the NRC documents I read. The SARs' limit is a **fuel temperature**, which the case already checks; plan 17 says so.
- The **gap**: standard TRIGA fuel is "non-gapped" (the cold gap about zero: the UT SAR Table 4-10 has a fuel diameter of 1.43 in, and the cladding 0.020 in thick, outside diameter 1.47 in, hence an inside diameter 1.43 in: **no gap**); pulsing opens one (Simnad section 3, page not noted: in standard TRIGA fuel the steady power temperature rises after pulsing, "attributed to the formation of a gas gap" from the fuel's rapid expansion in a pulse). The ACPR variant has a deliberate 0.01 in (0.25 mm; page 4-2) gap, or "375 microns, 0.015 in" in another sentence of the same report (section 3, page not noted: **the report is inconsistent with itself**). A fuel element in a pulse **swells the cladding by contact** after the initial gap is bridged (page 3-4): the cladding is "compelled to make provision" for the fuel's thermal expansion.
- Sandia FEA (a FREC-II gapped variant, **not** a standard TRIGA pin: gas gap 0.043 cm, 0.051 cm cladding, a zirconium rod, four dimples): a steady 1000 C fuel and a 154 C cladding, total gas pressure 2.44e6 Pa (the correlation of the library with X = 1.6, H/Zr 1.6, plus the backfill); the bulk cladding Mises stress 66 MPa (yield 200 MPa, UTS 510 MPa at 154 C) and up to 120 MPa; at the dimples 340 MPa outside and 700 MPa inside (plastic: the report concludes "permanent deformation of cladding dimples without failure", verified by radiography). A result FEED could be **compared with**, at least in order of magnitude: 66 MPa bulk hoop for 2.44 MPa pressure is the thin-shell estimate for the FREC-II radius, 1.78 cm / 0.051 cm: 2.44 x 35 = 85 MPa (the slide gives 66 MPa: the FEA, not the formula).
- **Conclusion: no published TRIGA stress calculation for a pulse at the level plan 17 builds was found.** The numbers a FEED result can be set against are the thin-shell estimate, the burst test (72 ksi at room temperature), the strengths above and the temperature limits.

## 6. U-ZrH fuel stress and strain limits

From the UT-Austin SAR 1991, Table 4-1, "Physical properties of delta phase U-ZrH" (page 4-2, read as an image; it is a one-number-per-quantity table): elastic modulus **9.1e6 psi (62.7 GPa) at 20 C and 6.0e6 psi (41.4 GPa) at 650 C**; **ultimate tensile strength (to 650 C) 24,000 psi (165 MPa)**; **compressive strength (20 C) 60,000 psi (414 MPa)**; compressive yield (20 C) 35,000 psi (241 MPa). (The library's `triga-uzrh-h16` and `docs/spike/mechanics-data.md` already hold the same numbers from the SARs.) No temperature curve of the strength, no strain limit, and no fuel failure criterion from a source I read. Simnad notes (section 2.11) that ZrH is brittle, creeps at high temperature and has a tensile-strength figure (Fig. 2-18, **not read numerically**); the dynamic modulus 63,000 MPa at room temperature falling to about 41,000 at 650 C (Ref. 17 there; measured on the hydride **without uranium**). So a fuel hoop or radial tensile stress **can be compared with 165 MPa as a stated design value**; the compressive strength of 414 MPa is the only other limit; **a tensile stress above the UTS means a cracked fuel** (which the TRIGA fuel is known to be after pulses: this is not a failure of the element). A comparison would be a teaching figure, not a failure criterion.

## What this changes in the plan

The wave transit across the fuel radius is 4.7 microseconds, 2000 times shorter than a millisecond rise. A warning with the numbers is enough (the plan's recommendation); a density need not be a library field to give it: a stated value with this source is enough, and the plan's "unverified" 6 microseconds can be called verified.
